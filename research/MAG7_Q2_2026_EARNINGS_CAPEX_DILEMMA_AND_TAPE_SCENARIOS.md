# Mag 7 Q2 2026 Earnings: Capex Dilemma, Accounting Optics, and Tape Scenarios

Prepared July 18, 2026, using the July 17 close.

This is a private scenario-research memo, not financial advice. Forecast ranges are judgmental and are not outputs of a validated trading model. The market, credit, oil, options, and consensus data should be refreshed before a position is changed.

## Bottom line

The hyperscalers are unlikely to rescue their free cash flow by cutting AI capex this earnings season. Their strategic incentives, contracted builds, customer backlogs, and the U.S. policy regime all point toward **reaffirming or modestly raising nominal capex**, while using operating growth, backlog, AI revenue disclosures, financing, and careful presentation to defend the spend.

That does **not** guarantee a durable rebound in hardware.

The market just received the hardware bull case and rejected it: TSMC reported record profit, raised its growth outlook, increased capex, and guided another strong quarter, yet semiconductors continued to fall. The current selloff is therefore no longer a simple question of whether AI demand exists. It is a question of:

1. how much of capex growth is real compute volume versus inflated memory/component prices;
2. whether AI revenue and gross profit are compounding faster than the new depreciation and financing burden;
3. whether today's scarcity margins become tomorrow's memory and capacity overbuild; and
4. whether investors will still pay peak multiples when excellent news no longer produces positive price response.

My base case is a **split earnings season**:

- Alphabet beats the published revenue bar, keeps Cloud growth above 50%, reports weak free cash flow, and reaffirms the $180-190 billion 2026 capex range with 2027 still higher.
- Microsoft produces the cleanest spend-to-revenue bridge and has the best odds of a durable positive reaction.
- Meta produces strong ad growth and probably guides capex toward the upper half of its existing range; it remains the highest-beta positive or negative reaction.
- Amazon shows solid AWS growth but remains the most exposed to the “great operating quarter, almost no free cash flow” objection.
- Apple remains the cash-conversion refuge, but its all-time-high setup makes a good report vulnerable to a sell-the-news response.
- Tesla's known delivery beat helps revenue, but a roughly $3 billion quarterly free-cash-flow deficit and more than $25 billion of 2026 capex leave the stock dependent on autonomy and Optimus narrative proof.
- Nvidia does not report until August 26. The July hyperscaler calls will be its demand pre-announcement, but even bullish capex commentary may produce only a relief rally in hardware.

The most likely aggregate outcome is **not “save hyperscalers” or “save hardware.”** It is that the strongest monetizers hold up, the weakest cash converters remain under pressure, and hardware disperses. Nvidia, networking, custom silicon, and advanced packaging can behave differently from commodity memory and NAND even when the same capex pool funds them.

## The first-principles mistake in the “capex up or capex down” binary

Nominal capex is not one variable. It is at least four:

> nominal capex = physical compute volume × unit price + long-duration buildings/power/network + finance-lease timing

And the economic return is:

> incremental AI gross profit - depreciation - energy - financing - model/data/talent expense

This creates four very different signals that can all appear as “capex went up”:

1. **Healthy volume growth:** more useful compute is installed against contracted demand.
2. **Price inflation:** the same or slightly more compute costs much more because HBM, DRAM, storage, and components are scarce.
3. **Mix shift:** spending moves from Nvidia GPUs toward TPU, Trainium, custom ASICs, networking, cooling, and power.
4. **Accounting/timing:** finance leases commence, construction payments land, or equipment is received in a different quarter.

Microsoft already said roughly $25 billion of its expected 2026 capex comes from higher component pricing. It also said roughly two-thirds of its latest quarterly capex was short-lived CPUs and GPUs, while the remainder funded longer-lived sites and infrastructure. Alphabet has described an approximately 60/40 split between servers and longer-duration data-center/networking assets. Amazon says data centers can have 30-plus-year lives while servers generally have five- to six-year useful lives.

Therefore:

- **Capex dollars up because memory prices rose** can be bad for hyperscaler returns without representing more hardware units.
- It can also be a late-cycle signal for memory stocks because peak current margins invite future capacity.
- **Capex dollars flat with more custom silicon and better utilization** can be good for hyperscaler ROIC while being bad for Nvidia or commodity memory.
- **Capex growth slowing while backlog stays strong** can be an efficiency signal, not a demand collapse.
- **Capex cut plus backlog/cloud deceleration** is the real break signal.

The only Goldilocks combination that can lift both buyers and suppliers durably is:

> real compute volume up + utilization high + AI/cloud revenue accelerating + gross profit growing faster than depreciation and financing cost

That is a much higher bar than “management raised capex.”

## Will they “pamper” the accounting?

They will almost certainly **pamper the presentation**. I do not expect evidence of coordinated or improper accounting.

The legal and predictable presentation tools are:

- emphasize backlog, annualized AI revenue, token/API growth, seats, and capacity constraints rather than current free cash flow;
- separate operating results from volatile private-investment gains and losses;
- discuss capex excluding or including finance leases depending on the comparison;
- emphasize that part of the spend is long-lived land, buildings, networking, and power rather than five-year accelerators;
- point to component-price inflation as a reason nominal capex rose without conceding that management ordered too much;
- present adjusted EPS, constant-currency growth, and “underlying” margins;
- finance the build with debt, preferreds, common equity, and leases so the cash burden is distributed across time and capital providers; and
- shift attention from quarterly FCF to operating cash flow, remaining performance obligations, future contracted revenue, or return on invested capital.

None of those removes the economics. Cash paid for property and equipment remains visible in the cash-flow statement. Depreciation arrives later. Debt and lease interest arrive later. Equity financing avoids near-term cash strain but creates dilution.

The current accounting distortions are already unusually large:

| Company | Latest headline distortion | What to use instead |
|---|---|---|
| Alphabet | Q1 other income was $37.7B versus $39.7B of operating income; EPS was $5.11 | Services/Cloud operating income, cash from operations, cash capex, and normalized EPS |
| Amazon | Q1 net income included a $16.8B pre-tax gain on Anthropic | AWS/retail operating income, quarterly and trailing FCF |
| Meta | Q1 included an $8.03B tax benefit; headline EPS was $10.44 | Approximately $7.31 EPS excluding the benefit, operating income, and FCF |
| Microsoft | Reports both GAAP and results adjusted for OpenAI investments; RPO changes materially when OpenAI is included | Azure growth, RPO excluding concentration effects, AI run-rate, and FCF |
| Tesla | Non-GAAP excludes stock compensation and digital-asset gains/losses | GAAP operating margin, automotive gross margin ex-credits, capex, and FCF |
| Nvidia | GAAP EPS has been affected by investment gains; starting FY27 its non-GAAP metrics include stock compensation | Revenue, gross margin, cash flow, and hyperscale versus ACIE revenue |

The market is likely to look through these items more aggressively this quarter because cash conversion, not headline EPS, is the disputed variable.

## Alphabet: the first and most important test

### Why the quarter should be operationally good

Alphabet entered Q2 with unusually strong operating momentum:

- Q1 revenue grew 22% to $109.9 billion.
- Search and Other grew 19%.
- Cloud grew 63% to $20.0 billion.
- Cloud operating income rose to $6.6 billion.
- Cloud backlog nearly doubled sequentially to more than $460 billion, with roughly half expected to be recognized within 24 months.
- First-party model APIs were processing 19 billion tokens per minute by the June capital-raise announcement.

The EU decision and the delayed Gemini 3.5 Pro launch matter for the multiple and for long-run competitive confidence, but they should not destroy the June-quarter revenue print. The EU measures begin affecting Android and search-data access on a later timetable. The model delay is a product-execution warning, especially in coding, not a retroactive Q2 revenue shock.

The market's claim that Google has no model that “beats Claude” should be narrowed. Benchmark and task leadership are unstable and model-specific. What matters for this earnings call is whether Google converts its full stack into paid Cloud consumption, Search monetization, subscriptions, and lower serving cost. A coding-model delay is negative, but it is not the same as weak Cloud demand.

### Why free cash flow should be ugly

Q1 already showed the bridge: $45.8 billion of operating cash flow minus $35.7 billion of capex left $10.1 billion of FCF. Q2 capex is supposed to ramp, and Alphabet has historically had heavier cash-tax payments in Q2. A quarter with roughly $44-48 billion of capex can produce FCF close to zero even if operating income is excellent.

That is why I expect management to emphasize:

- Cloud backlog and the timing of its conversion;
- Search/AI Overview monetization;
- token/API usage;
- paid Gemini and Google One subscriptions;
- the portion of capex that is demand-backed;
- custom TPU cost advantages; and
- the capital raise as balanced financing rather than distress.

### The likely capex answer

A cut is unlikely. Alphabet announced in June that 2026 capex remains $180-190 billion and 2027 capex should increase significantly. It simultaneously announced $80 billion of common equity, mandatory convertible preferreds, a Berkshire private placement, and a future ATM program. Management does not raise that capital and then reverse the build six weeks later absent a severe demand shock.

My expected language is: demand exceeds capacity, capex remains in the stated range, supply/component pricing and payment timing create quarterly variability, and 2027 remains higher. The company may avoid raising the range because the equity raise and recent share weakness already created dilution sensitivity.

### What makes the stock go up or down

The July 24 at-the-money option straddle implied roughly a 6.6% move after the July 17 close.

Bull reaction requires most of the following:

- Cloud growth at least in the mid-50s;
- backlog above $500 billion or another clearly strong sequential increase;
- Search growth holding in the mid-teens or better;
- Cloud margin holding near or above 30% despite depreciation;
- 2026 capex range not raised; and
- a credible explanation of Gemini product timing.

Bear reaction can occur even with a headline beat if:

- Cloud growth falls below 50%;
- backlog growth slows sharply;
- Q2 FCF is negative without a clean tax/timing bridge;
- 2027 capex is pushed higher again;
- management leans on token counts without revenue or margin evidence; or
- the equity ATM/dilution plan expands.

My base call is a good operating print, poor FCF, and a volatile initial reaction that ultimately depends on the Cloud/backlog combination. The EU and model headlines lower the market's willingness to accept vague answers.

## Company-by-company incentive analysis

### Microsoft: spend because monetization is most visible

Microsoft has the strongest defense of the group: $37 billion AI annual revenue run-rate growing 123% in the latest quarter, more than 20 million paid Microsoft 365 Copilot seats, Azure growth near 40%, and $627 billion of commercial RPO including OpenAI.

Its interest is to prove that capex converts into an annuity. It will keep distinguishing short-lived GPUs/CPUs from 15-year sites and emphasizing that it remains capacity constrained. The weak point is customer concentration: RPO including OpenAI is much more impressive than the underlying commercial-bookings growth excluding OpenAI. Watch both versions.

Expected verdict: **the best hyperscaler print and the best chance of holding gains after the call**.

### Meta: spend because the ad engine funds the option

Meta's core business already shows direct AI return through ranking, engagement, ad conversion, creative tools, and pricing. Q1 ad impressions rose 19% and price per ad rose 12%. That gives Meta a monetization bridge that does not require a third-party cloud business.

This week's 5GW Hyperion expansion makes a capex cut especially unlikely. Reports that Meta may sell surplus compute and hosted model access are strategically logical but remain reported plans, not a disclosed material revenue line. The positive interpretation is monetization flexibility; the negative interpretation is evidence that Meta over-ordered.

Expected verdict: **revenue beat, capex near the upper half, strong but volatile stock reaction**.

### Amazon: spend because AWS cannot concede capacity

AWS grew 28% in Q1, its fastest rate in 15 quarters. Amazon's custom-chip business exceeded a $20 billion annual run-rate, and commitments from Anthropic and OpenAI support large future capacity needs. Amazon cannot protect near-term FCF by ceding AI cloud capacity to Microsoft and Google.

The problem is cash conversion. Q1 quarterly FCF was approximately negative $17 billion and TTM FCF was only $1.2 billion. Amazon will likely emphasize operating cash flow, Trainium economics, AWS growth, and long-term ROIC rather than FCF.

Expected verdict: **solid fundamentals, weak cash, and the highest risk that a respectable beat is not enough**.

### Apple: spend very little because distribution is the moat

Apple's interest is almost the mirror image. It can rent frontier-model intelligence, integrate it into Siri and Apple Intelligence, and monetize through devices and Services. Its six-month property purchases were only $4.3 billion against $82.6 billion of operating cash flow.

That is why Apple became the rotation sponge during the hardware rout. It is an AI beneficiary without the same frontier-infrastructure burden. The risk is valuation and positioning: a capital-light model is now widely recognized, the stock is at an all-time high, and the next earnings beat may already be pre-bought.

Expected verdict: **best business-model optics, but not necessarily the best post-earnings trade**.

### Nvidia and hardware vendors: defend orders, not hyperscaler returns

Hardware vendors want customers to maintain orders, sign longer commitments, and accept scarcity pricing. Their interest is not the same as hyperscaler ROIC. Memory suppliers benefit today from price inflation that makes buyer capex less productive.

Nvidia is better positioned than commodity memory because it owns the software/platform layer and is splitting disclosure between hyperscale and broader AI-cloud/industrial/enterprise demand. But the July selloff shows that even record current growth may not offset fear of customer custom silicon, 2027 capacity additions, and margin normalization.

Expected verdict: **capex reaffirmation produces a tradable relief rally, but hardware does not regain durable leadership unless price starts responding positively to strong data**.

## Iran, oil, the administration, and the midterm hypothesis

### What is supported

The administration has a clear strategic interest in continued domestic AI buildout:

- federal policy accelerates data-center permitting;
- national-security directives call for advanced AI and high-security compute capacity;
- the White House has framed AI infrastructure as a competition with China; and
- the Ratepayer Protection Pledge asks hyperscalers to build, bring, or buy the energy needed for their data centers so households do not absorb the cost.

That policy mix supports capex but changes its composition. More spending may flow into behind-the-meter power, gas generation, grid upgrades, cooling, and long-duration sites. It does not guarantee the same marginal dollar goes to Nvidia or commodity memory.

The Iran/oil shock is also real now, not merely a possibility for next week. U.S. and Iranian strikes intensified, Hormuz traffic remained restricted, Red Sea risk increased, and Brent rose about 16% in five sessions. Additional U.S. refueling aircraft and reported wider military options are conditions that keep the tail risk elevated.

### What is not supported

There is no reliable evidence that the administration is engineering an equity correction to reduce inflation before the midterms. The repo's own election-cycle work rejects the midterm calendar as a standalone signal and treats it only as a risk modulator.

The Iran-war version of the theory is also backwards in first-order inflation terms:

- more war risk raises oil, gasoline, shipping, insurance, and potentially electricity costs;
- those channels hit headline inflation and consumer sentiment much faster than a tech-stock wealth effect reduces demand; and
- higher energy prices can keep real yields restrictive, which is especially negative for long-duration growth equities.

The disciplined approach is conditional, not an administration-intent forecast:

- **If Hormuz/Red Sea shipping worsens and Brent holds above the mid-$80s or moves through $90**, even good earnings can be sold because inflation and real-yield risk dominate.
- **If strikes de-escalate and shipping normalizes**, oil can retrace quickly, creating a powerful relief tailwind for tech into the second week of earnings.
- **If credit spreads remain near 2.7% and equal-weight equities hold up**, the event remains factor de-grossing.
- **If high-yield spreads move decisively above 3%, HYG breaks, and equal-weight/banks join the decline**, the thesis upgrades to broader risk contagion.

## What would actually save both hyperscalers and hardware

Do not use the capex headline alone. Use this four-part scorecard on every call:

| Test | Bull evidence | Bear evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | backlog/RPO and recognized cloud revenue both accelerate | only backlog or token counts rise while recognized revenue slows |
| Capex quality | higher spend is tied to volume, utilization, and contracted customers | higher spend is mostly component inflation, leases, or vague “future opportunity” |
| Unit economics | cloud/AI gross profit rises faster than depreciation and energy | revenue grows but gross margin and FCF deteriorate faster |
| Financing | debt/equity/leases fund high-return assets without worsening per-share economics | dilution and debt rise because internal cash cannot cover low-visibility builds |

A nominal capex raise with three bull columns can stabilize both groups. A capex raise with three bear columns can crush both. A cut with strong demand and efficiency can help hyperscalers while hurting hardware. A cut with weak demand hurts everyone.

## The trading and allocation conclusion

My call for this earnings season is:

1. **Do not assume a hyperscaler capex cut.** The probability is lower than the tape implies because recent financing, announced projects, backlog, and strategic competition lock in the build.
2. **Do not assume capex reaffirmation saves memory.** TSMC already demonstrated that strong demand and a higher capex outlook are insufficient when positioning and future-cycle fears dominate.
3. **Prefer monetization proof over capex beta.** Microsoft is the cleanest, Meta the highest beta, Alphabet the most important first test, Amazon the weakest cash converter.
4. **Treat Apple as a crowded refuge, not a free lunch.** Its business-model advantage is real; the earnings-trade hurdle is also high.
5. **Treat Nvidia better than the memory/NAND complex, but not as immune.** Its August print can be exceptional and the stock can still derate if 2027 demand durability is questioned.
6. **Treat neoclouds as the fracture line.** If hyperscalers internalize more compute, resell excess capacity, or obtain cheaper financing, the levered intermediaries face utilization, refinancing, and residual-value pressure.
7. **Keep July and October separate.** This is tranche one of the repo's FCF/monetization trough thesis. One week of earnings cannot resolve the full H2 2026-H1 2027 depreciation and cash-conversion cycle.

The most likely near-term shape is **good operating earnings, ugly cash flow, high capex, and violent dispersion**. That can prevent a full Mag 7 collapse without restoring the hardware bubble's prior breadth.

## Falsifiers and live checklist

The base case is wrong if two or more of the following occur:

- Alphabet Cloud growth falls below 50% and backlog growth stalls.
- Microsoft Azure constant-currency growth falls below 39% or the AI revenue run-rate stops accelerating.
- Meta raises capex above $145 billion without a stronger revenue/operating-income guide.
- AWS growth falls below 27% while Amazon keeps the $200 billion capex plan.
- Two hyperscalers cut real unit/capacity plans, not merely report timing changes.
- High-yield OAS rises above 3% and equal-weight indexes start confirming QQQ weakness.
- Strong hyperscaler capex commentary fails to produce even a short hardware relief rally; that would indicate a deeper valuation/cycle break.

The bull case strengthens if:

- Alphabet Cloud backlog exceeds $500 billion and Cloud margin remains above 30%.
- Microsoft pairs Azure acceleration with a materially higher AI revenue run-rate and stable full-year margins.
- Meta's ad growth stays above the mid-20s while capex remains inside the existing range.
- AWS reaches or exceeds 30% growth and raises forward operating-income guidance.
- hardware begins rising on good news rather than merely falling less.
- oil retraces and credit remains calm.
